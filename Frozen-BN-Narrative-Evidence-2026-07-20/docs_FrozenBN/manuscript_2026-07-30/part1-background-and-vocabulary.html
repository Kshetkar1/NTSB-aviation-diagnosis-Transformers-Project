Part 1 — Background and Vocabulary

This part gives you everything you need to read the rest: what the data is, what a Bayesian network is, and the handful of machine-learning and statistics terms the project depends on. Nothing here assumes prior knowledge.

1.1 The data: what NTSB records look like

The National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) investigates every civil aviation accident in the United States. For each accident it publishes a record with two very different kinds of content:

ContentWhat it isExample
Coded fields Structured, machine-readable facts chosen from fixed menus by trained investigators: occurrence types, flight phase, findings/causes, injury level, damage level. damage = SUBS (substantial), injury = SERS (serious), finding = "landing gear, main gear strut"
Narrative Free-text prose describing what happened, written by the investigator. "During the landing roll, the right main landing gear collapsed and the airplane veered off the runway…"
Vocabulary — coded vs. free text. "Coded" data is what statistical models are traditionally built from, because every accident answers the same questions the same way. The narrative contains far richer detail, but historically it was unusable by probability models because no two narratives are structured alike. This project is about closing exactly that gap.

Where the data lives in this repository: the merged, cleaned dataset is shared/data/processed/refined_dataset.json (all years), and shared/data/processed/refined_dataset_1982_2006.json is the 1,742-accident window used to build the network (more on that window in Part 2). Path configuration: shared/code/config.py.

1.2 The model: what a Bayesian network is

Vocabulary — Bayesian network (BN). A directed graph where each node is a variable (e.g. "Fire", "Flight phase", "Injury level"), each node has a small set of states (Fire: yes/no; Injury: fatal/serious/minor/none), and arrows encode which variables directly influence which. Attached to every node is a conditional probability table (CPT): the probability of each of its states given each combination of its parents' states. Multiply all the CPTs together and you get a full joint probability distribution over every variable at once.
Vocabulary — prior and posterior. The prior is what the network believes before you tell it anything about a specific accident — essentially historical base rates (e.g. "42.6% of accidents in this data have damage = NONE"). When you feed in facts about a specific case, the network updates every probability; the updated numbers are the posterior. The updating process is called inference.
Vocabulary — evidence. Anything you tell the network about a specific case. Hard evidence pins a node to one state with certainty ("there WAS a fire"). Soft / virtual evidence expresses partial confidence ("70% sure the phase was landing") — the mechanism for this, due to Judea Pearl, attaches a temporary "observation" node whose CPT encodes your confidence. This distinction matters enormously later, because narrative-derived facts are rarely certain.

Why would anyone want this instead of a normal machine-learning classifier? Because a BN is a model of the whole situation, not a single input→output function. One network answers every question — P(fire)? P(severe injury | icing + night)? What changes if we assume the gear had not collapsed? — and every answer decomposes into human-readable probability tables estimated from real accidents. A trained classifier answers exactly one question and shows you nothing.

Our network-construction code: Frozen-BN-Narrative-Evidence-2026-07-20/code/bn_upgraded.py (builds the multi-state network from the coded window data, using the pyAgrum library). The original network it follows is from Zhang & Mahadevan, Reliability Engineering & System Safety 209 (2021) 107371.

1.3 The two tasks: prognosis and diagnosis — two readouts of one model

The goal is not just to predict injury and damage. Given a narrative typed by a user, the question is bidirectional: what are the likely downstream outcomes (prognosis), and what are the likely upstream causes (diagnosis) — all of them, not a pre-chosen pair?

TaskQuestionOur target variables
Prognosis (severity) Given the facts of an accident, how bad are the outcomes? Injury level (fatal / serious / minor / none) and aircraft damage (destroyed / substantial / minor / none) — a 4-way choice each.
Diagnosis (causes) Which causes — events, findings, persons — become more probable? Every upstream node in the network; scored at the level of the four official top-level cause categories: Personnel, Aircraft, Environment, Organizational (why the scoring is at that level: Part 6).
Vocabulary — one joint posterior, two readouts. These are not two separate systems. A Bayesian network holds a joint distribution over all its variables, so once narrative-derived evidence is entered and propagated, every node — upstream cause nodes and downstream severity nodes alike — has an updated posterior in the same inference. Prognosis and diagnosis are two scored views of that one update: Part 5 scores the two severity nodes, Part 6 scores the upstream cause nodes (rolled up to four categories). One honesty note, expanded in Part 4: the headline severity number additionally uses a dedicated severity-evidence channel (the k-NN readout), because entering event evidence and severity evidence from the same narrative into one inference double-counts it.
Vocabulary — a caution about the word "prognosis". NTSB narratives are written after the investigation, so nobody has this text before the outcome is known. Our severity task is therefore not real-time prediction; it is a test of whether the text's mechanism description carries the outcome signal — useful for triage of incoming reports, coding assistance, and consistency checking. We say this precisely in the paper (docs_FrozenBN/RESULTS_SECTION.md) because sloppy wording here is the kind of thing reviewers reject.

1.4 The text machinery: embeddings and retrieval

Vocabulary — embedding. A function that turns a piece of text into a long list of numbers (a vector) such that texts with similar meaning get vectors that point in similar directions. We use an OpenAI text-embedding model (configured in shared/code/config.py, used in shared/code/main_app.py). Two narratives about gear collapses on landing end up close together even if they share few exact words.
Vocabulary — cosine similarity and k-NN retrieval. Cosine similarity measures the angle between two vectors — 1.0 means "same direction" (very similar meaning). Retrieval means: embed a new narrative, compare it against the pre-computed embeddings of all ~1,700 historical window narratives, and return the top-k most similar accidents (we use k = 100 by default). Those k accidents are the "nearest neighbors" (k-NN) of the new one.
Vocabulary — TF-IDF. A much older, simpler text representation: count each word (and word pair), weighted so that rare words count more. No neural network involved. It matters in this project because a TF-IDF model turned out to be a surprisingly strong competitor — see Part 5 and Part 7.

Embedding + retrieval implementation: shared/code/main_app.py (functions get_embedding — with a disk cache at shared/data/processed/query_emb_cache.npz so reruns are deterministic and free — and find_top_matches). The pre-computed window index: shared/data/processed/embeddings_1982_2006.npy with its map embeddings_map_1982_2006.json.

1.5 The evaluation vocabulary: how you prove any of this works

Vocabulary — held-out evaluation. The cardinal rule of testing a model: score it on data it has never seen. Our network and retrieval index are built ONLY from 1982–2006 accidents; all testing happens on 2007–2019 accidents (296 of them for severity). If you test on the data you built from, you are grading a student on questions they memorized.
Vocabulary — leakage. Any way test-set information sneaks into the system. The most dangerous kind here: NTSB narratives often state the outcome in the text ("the airplane was destroyed", "the passenger received fatal injuries"). A model reading that sentence isn't predicting — it's copying the answer. Part 7 is entirely about how we found, removed, and audited this.
Vocabulary — baseline. A deliberately simple competitor that defines what "good" means. Ours range from trivial (always guess the most common outcome) to strong (supervised logistic regression on embeddings). A result only matters relative to baselines.
Vocabulary — supervised vs. zero-parameter. A supervised model is fitted to labeled examples (narrative → known severity) — it needs coded labels to exist. Our chain is zero-parameter for this task: nothing anywhere is fitted to narrative→severity pairs; the network's tables come from coded data alone and the retrieval step just measures similarity. That asymmetry is the fair-fight caveat we state whenever we compare against supervised baselines.
