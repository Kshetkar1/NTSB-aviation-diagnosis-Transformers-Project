Narratives as Evidence for a Frozen Bayesian Network

A start-to-finish manuscript of the approach — written for a reader who is new to the field. Every claim cites the file in this repository where it lives, and every number cites the test that produced it.

What this project is, in three sentences

Zhang & Mahadevan (2021) built a Bayesian network — a probabilistic model of how aviation accidents unfold — from NTSB's hand-coded accident records for 1982–2006. We reproduce that network exactly, freeze it (never retrain it), and then show that the free-text narrative of a brand-new accident can be converted into evidence the frozen network understands — so the network can predict injury and damage severity and suggest cause categories for accidents it has never seen, with zero training. On 296 held-out accidents from 2007–2019, this chain reaches 90.9% injury / 77.4% damage accuracy — statistically tied with supervised machine-learning models trained on labeled data — while keeping everything a trained black box cannot offer: full probabilistic reasoning, what-if queries, and explanations that trace back to real historical accidents.

The parts

Part 1 — Background and vocabulary

What NTSB data looks like, what a Bayesian network is, and every term you need (nodes, CPTs, priors, posteriors, evidence, embeddings, held-out testing, leakage) explained from zero.

Part 2 — Reproducing Zhang: the foundation

Why we spent weeks reproducing someone else's 2021 paper to the digit before doing anything new: 102 fire accidents, P(fire) = 5.527942×10⁻⁷, and all 85 rows of his Table 7 — plus the 85-vs-113 lesson in honest claiming.

Part 3 — The journey: what we tried first and why we switched

Four earlier approaches, in order, each with the reason it was superseded or rejected: embedding-counting (March), CPT upgrades (June), the LLM-first parser (July), and structural re-ranking. The dated folders in this repository are the receipts.

Part 4 — The pipeline: how a narrative becomes evidence

The accepted architecture, step by step: redaction, the deterministic parser (hard evidence), retrieval of similar accidents (soft evidence), the k-NN severity readout (virtual evidence), and why fusing everything at once backfires (the double-counting negative result).

Part 5 — Evaluating severity: the numbers and what they mean

The held-out experiment design, every metric explained (accuracy, Macro-F1, Brier, McNemar, Holm, bootstrap CIs, sensitivity/specificity), the three supervised baselines we attack ourselves with, and the full results table.

Part 6 — Evaluating diagnosis: cause categories across a taxonomy break

NTSB changed its coding system in 2008, making naive comparison impossible. The era-fair rollup to four cause categories, the mapping audit, and the results — including the negative result we report on purpose.

Part 7 — Leakage, audits, and honest claims

The most important chapter for trusting everything else: how outcome text leaked into early numbers (93/81 → 90.9/77.4), the redaction protocol and its precise scope, the leak probe that became a baseline, and the limitations we disclose instead of hide.
