Part 5 — Evaluating Severity: the Numbers and What They Mean

5.1 The experiment design

Test set: every accident in the corpus outside the 1982–2006 window with a factual narrative of at least 100 characters — 296 accidents from 2007–2019, listed one by one in outputs/cohort_manifest.json. Truth: the NTSB-coded injury level (via Zhang's derivation rule, zhang_injury_code in code/prognosis.py) and coded damage level. Task: 4-way classification for each target. Rule that makes comparisons fair: every predictor and every baseline is scored on the identical 296 accidents with identical text treatment (4,000-character truncation + redaction) — the manifest script cross-checks this and reports true/false per pairing.

5.2 The metrics, each in one honest paragraph

Accuracy (top-1). The fraction of accidents where the model's highest- probability class equals the coded class. Intuitive, but it flatters models under class imbalance: 58.4% of held-out accidents have injury = NONE, so a model that always says NONE scores 58.4% while knowing nothing. That is exactly our prior baseline — which is why accuracy is never reported alone.
Macro-F1. Compute F1 (the balance of precision and recall) separately for each of the 4 classes, then average without weighting by class size — so the rare fatal class counts as much as the common none class. A model that ignores rare classes gets punished here even if its accuracy looks fine. The always-NONE baseline's Macro-F1 is 0.184 (injury) / 0.149 (damage).
Brier score. Squared error of the full predicted probability distribution against the true outcome — it rewards being confidently right and punishes being confidently wrong, unlike accuracy which only looks at the top choice. Lower is better.
Bootstrap 95% confidence interval. Resample the 296 accidents with replacement 10,000 times, recompute the accuracy each time, and report the middle 95% of the results. It answers: "given only 296 test cases, how much could this number wobble?" Seeded (seed 42) so reruns are identical.
McNemar exact test. The right significance test for comparing two classifiers on the same items. It ignores accidents where both models are right or both wrong, and asks: among the discordant accidents (exactly one model right), is the split more lopsided than coin flips would explain? The p-value is the probability of a split at least this lopsided under a fair coin.
Holm–Bonferroni correction. If you run many significance tests, some come up "significant" by luck. Holm's procedure inflates each p-value based on how many tests are in the family, controlling the overall false-alarm rate. We designate five primary comparisons per target (bn-sev vs each baseline and vs its own ablation) and correct within that family; every other comparison is labeled exploratory and never claimed as significant. Honest wording note: the primaries were designated during the study, not pre-registered before data collection — the report says so.
Sensitivity / specificity (the severe-outcome screen). Collapse the 4 classes into severe (fatal+serious, or destroyed+substantial) vs not. Sensitivity = fraction of truly severe accidents flagged severe; specificity = fraction of non-severe accidents correctly cleared. This is the operating view a safety analyst actually cares about.

5.3 The baselines — including the two that almost beat us

Why we attack our own result with strong baselines. A reviewer's first question about any fancy pipeline is "would a simple supervised model do just as well?" If the authors don't answer it, the reviewer assumes the worst. So we built three supervised competitors ourselves, on the identical cohort, and report every result including the uncomfortable ones.
BaselineWhat it isSource file
lr (parsed features) Logistic regression on the SAME parsed-evidence vector the network consumes, trained on the 1,286 window narratives with labels. tests/lr_baseline_heldout.py

How to read this honestly — the paper's actual claim. Severity accuracy from mechanism text is, empirically, a commodity: three different supervised models and our zero-parameter chain all land in one statistical tie. The claim is therefore NOT "we predict better." It is: a frozen, validated network driven by free text matches supervised models without any training, while additionally providing joint probabilistic reasoning, evidence composition, what-if queries, and audit-ready explanations that none of the trained models possess. bn-sev also has the best damage Macro-F1 (0.697) and the best damage severe-screen sensitivity (75.3%, vs 67.9% embedding / 58.0% TF-IDF) — the operating views where being wrong is expensive.

5.5 The failures, disclosed

What the model misses. The rare classes. Of 3 fatal accidents, only 1 is flagged severe on injury (predicted serious); the other 2 are predicted NONE on injury — though both are flagged severe on damage (destroyed / substantial), so the screen does not wave them through entirely. Minor injuries (16 cases) are never ranked top-1 either. The cause is class rarity in this window (3 of 296), not a fixable bug; the confusion matrices are published in full in outputs/heldout_significance.md and the limitation is stated in the paper. A note for the reader: an earlier README sentence claimed all 3 fatals were flagged severe — the per-item data proved it false and it was corrected. Check claims against outputs/heldout_per_item.json, not against prose.

5.6 Hyperparameters: the k question

The only knob in the chain is k, the neighbor count (default 100 — the pipeline's pre-set retrieval width). An internal validation slice (2002–2006) mildly favored k = 25; a held-out ablation at k = 25 scores 89.9% / 77.7% vs 90.9% / 77.4% at k = 100 — a flat plateau, so the choice does not matter and nothing was tuned on test data. Sweep: tests/retrieval_hyperparam_sensitivity.py → outputs/hyperparam_sensitivity.md; k=25 ablation report: outputs/heldout_significance_k25.md; the full "what is fitted anywhere" inventory: docs_FrozenBN/FREE_PARAMETERS.md.
