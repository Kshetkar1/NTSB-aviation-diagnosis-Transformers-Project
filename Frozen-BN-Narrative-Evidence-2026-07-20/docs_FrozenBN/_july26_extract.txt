[14] (List Paragraph) The initial full LLM attempt and why it failed
[15] (Normal) When initially attempting this research, the LLM approach was created to read the accident reports and produce the probabilities itself. After running the approach, here are the results. First, LLM probabilities are not grounded in data, and they hallucinate on the probabilities [5]. Second, it was too confident in its probability outputs, and they don’t back it up with real incidents. Third, a small adjustment to the same query completely changes the predicted probabilities. LLMs are good at retrieving and structuring text, but reliable probabilities have to be grounded in the real data. Therefore, we utilize the LLM only to interpret text, while the probabilities are data grounded probabilistic and can be calculated from the database.

[101] (List Paragraph) Diagnosis
[102] (Normal) Diagnosis mode predicts what the likely cause is given the query. After the user types in their query and the embeddings are created and tested against the full NTSB embedding space and clusters are created, then the diagnosis part happens. We take the probability of each cluster given the query P(K | Q). We compute the similarity weights. Each weight is the average similarity times the number of incidents in the cluster. Then the cluster that is both similar to the query with many related incidents gets more weight.
[103] (Normal) Formula: P(K|Q) = (Average similarity_K x n_K) / sum of that weight over all active clusters.
[104] (Normal) Then we perform P(C | K) which is the probability of a cause given a cluster. This part is a frequency count. We count how often a cause appears and divide it by the total causes in that cluster. P(C | K) is the count of the causes in the cluster divided by the total causes in the cluster. P(C | Q) is the final probability result using the law of total probability.
[105] (Normal) Formula: P(C | Q) = _K  P(C | K) x P( K | Q)
[106] (Normal) We sum each of the causes across all the clusters and then rank the causes by the P(C | Q). The output is the top-N cause with their probabilities.
[107] (List Paragraph) Prognosis
[108] (Normal) Prognosis predicts the most likely next event given the query. It uses the same math as diagnosis but has a different target. The anchor is the defining event. Each incident’s sequence of events has the defining event (the anchor event in the timeline of the incident). The next event is the event that comes immediately after the defining event in that incident sequence. We only use the incidents that have an event after the defining event. The probabilities are parallel to diagnosis mode. We use P(K | Q) the same similarity weighted cluster.
[109] (Normal) Then what is different is that we compute the P (Next event | K). We do a frequency count on how often each next event appears and divide by the total next events in that cluster. Then we perform the law of total probability on P (Next event | Q).
[110] (Normal) Formula: P(Next event | Q) = _K  P(Next event | K) x P(K | Q)
[111] (List Paragraph) The narrative to evidence parser
[112] (Normal) Waiting for coded data may take a long time to process and letting an LLM fully implement the probabilities result in results may not be trustworthy. Instead, the LLM only reads the text. The first step is the deterministic parser that does not use an LLM. This step looks for the exact words/phrases from the NTSB code dictionary in the narratives. Once found, a number between 0-1 is given to that number to signify how similar those words/phrases are to the NTSB code dictionary. The next step only happens if the first step is not able to find any words/phrase. This step utilizes the LLM to look at the 100 most similar past accidents to see if this fact actually appears in the coded records. [157] (Normal) Abstract:
[158] (Normal) Aviation safety is a critical concern for global air transportation systems. In this paper, we analyze aviation incident reports from 1982-2016 from the NTSB (National Transportation Safety Board). Two similar methodologies are created to capture the backward inference and forward inference of an incident report provided by a pilot or investigator. Initially, the user enters a query for their specific incident. Next, these narratives turn into embeddings to locate the distance between the query incident and other incidents using cosine similarity. Then, the top 50 most related incidents are grouped by their precomputed K-means clusters (clustering is run offline on the full corpus), each cluster labeled by an LLM. We utilize the law of total probability to determine the final probability for the cause of that queried incident. In prognosis mode, we repeat the same steps to reach the clustering pool. We then analyze the sequence of events data to determine the defining event. Once identified, we apply the law of total probability to get the top next events and probabilities. Another process for reranking the embeddings is also created for comparison to gauge that the pipeline is not displaying the causes based on related text, but by what caused the incident. To visualize this, we develop a computer program utilizing a streamlit application to build interactive web apps. These findings may help pilots and investigators use historical accident reports to receive probabilistic reasoning for specific query-based incidents. This study proposes that by searching through NTSB’s historical aviation database and utilizing their unstructured text, our methodology can predict the upstream causes of incidents and downstream next events. The inspiration for this alternative aviation safety assessment utilizing unstructured text narratives derives from the “Bayesian network modeling of accident investigation reports for aviation safety assessment” pioneered by Zhang and Mahadevan, 2021.

[197] (List Paragraph) Initial LLM Only Approach
[199] (List Paragraph) 5.1 Pipeline
[200] (Normal) The first step in our pipeline is the user types in their incident query. This is the description of what happened. The next step was retrieving similar cases. This was a hybrid approach. We utilized vector embeddings and keyword extraction to match the NTSB event codes, and the cases that were retrieved show their narratives and their codes. The following step was analyzing the context. Here we prompted an LLM to read the queries and retrieve all the narratives with the contextual factors (weather, human factors, flight phase, or maintenance). The next step was implementing the diagnosis mode to identify the upstream candidate causes, rank them by frequency in similar cases, and input preventative strategies based on the LLM’s knowledge.
[201] (Normal) For implementing prognosis mode, we started from the precursor and built the probabilistic progression chain with intervention points. At the end, for both diagnosis and prognosis paths, we utilized an LLM to recommend preventative actions in the moment and in the future and created a cause-and-effect mermaid diagram chain for the users to see the progression.
[204] (Normal) The next step is retrieval. We utilize an LLM and implement prompting to look at all 14 files and decide what incidents were similar based on keywords. The following step was factor extraction. The LLM read through all the narratives and retrieved all the context from the narratives like contributing factors, weather, and personnel issues using prompting.
[205] (List Paragraph) 5.2 Limitations
[207] (List Paragraph) With this Approach, there were a few issues that were unearthed. The first limitation is that the LLM does not calculate probabilities in that structured way. It infers from learned language patterns. The second limitation is that the outputs that sound confident are not supported by the data. It hallucinates. Even when retrieved, there is still overconfidence. The last limitation is how sensitive the phrasing is in the pipeline. Changing a single word can change the whole pipeline.
[209] (List Paragraph) 5.3 Lesson
[211] (List Paragraph) The main takeaway is that the LLM implemented the scoring and produced the probabilities itself but were ungrounded and unreproducible. We needed to ground our probabilities and data, so we were not getting hallucinated data that we could rely on. The lessons learned here are what helped mold the new methodology in Section 6 and Section 7.
[213] (Normal) 6 Proposed Methodology
[214] (Normal) 6.1 Merged Dataset
[215] (Normal) The data is scattered into eight different files: Aircraft, sequence of events, engines, findings, injury, events, narratives, and the code dictionary. To organize this, all files are merged on EV_ID, a data column that represents a particular incident, the files are merged into a new dataset JSON file. This allows for faster retrieval. The data has many mentions of the same EV_ID. This is to show that there can be multiple findings, engines, sequence of events, etc., but EV_ID numbers are unique for an incident. This merged dataset created 2,243 incidents with all related data to each incident.
[216] (Normal) 6.2 Embeddings
[217] (Normal) The narratives, codes/findings, and code dictionary were turned into Embeddings to find how close each narrative was to the query embedding.  Each text was embedded using OpenAI’s text_embedding_3_small model that creates 1,536-dimensional vectors. Each of these categories describe different parts of the incident. The narratives describe the full story, codes/findings show the contributing factors, and the code dictionary is a master list of all aviation event types.
[219] (Normal) 6.3 Similarity Search
[220] (Normal) When a query for an incident is written, it gets embedded with the same model (text_embedding_3_small). We compare the query embedding to every embedding in the corpus using cosine similarity. Cosine similarity measures the angles between two vectors. The smaller the angles, the more similar the text are to one another. We take the top 50 most similar incidents, and those top 50 get passed to the next step.
[221] (Normal) 6.4 Clustering
[222] (Normal) We group the incident embeddings into 50 clusters using K-means. This is run once offline and not at query time. The random seed = 42 so we can reproduce the results. Each cluster is named from the LLM (gpt-4o-mini), using the five narratives closest to the cluster center as context. At query time, the top 50 retrieved incidents are grouped by their cluster labels, and the results show clusters failure themes for that query.
[223] (Normal) 6.5 Diagnosis
[224] (Normal) Diagnosis mode predicts what the likely cause was given the query. After the user types in their query and the embeddings are created and tested against the full NTSB embedding space and clusters are created, then the diagnosis part happens. We take the probability of each cluster given the query P(K | Q). We compute the similarity weights. Each weight is the average similarity times the number of incidents in the cluster. Then the cluster that is both similar to the query with many related incidents gets more weight.
[225] (Normal) Formula: P(K|Q) = (Average similarity_K x n_K) / sum of that weight over all active clusters.
[226] (Normal) Then we perform P(C | K) which is the probability of a cause given a cluster. This part is a frequency count. We count how often a cause appears and divide it by the total causes in that cluster. P(C | K) is the count of the causes in the cluster divided by the total causes in the cluster. P(C | Q) is the final probability result using the law of total probability.
[227] (Normal) Formula: P(C | Q) = _K  P(C | K) x P( K | Q)
[228] (Normal) We sum each of the causes across all the clusters and then rank the causes by the P(C | Q). The output is the top-N cause with their probabilities.
[229] (Normal) 6.6 Prognosis
[230] (Normal) Prognosis predicts the most likely next event given the query. It uses the same math as diagnosis but has a different target. The anchor is the defining event. Each incident’s sequence of events has the defining event (the anchor event in the timeline of the incident). The next event is the event that comes immediately after the defining event in that incident sequence. We only use the incidents that have an event after the defining event. The probabilities are parallel to diagnosis mode. We use P(K | Q) the same similarity weighted cluster.
[231] (Normal) Prognosis differs from Diagnosis in that we compute the P (Next event | K). We do a frequency count on how often each next event appears and divide by the total next events in that cluster. Then we perform the law of total probability on P (Next event | Q).
[232] (Normal) Formula: P(Next event | Q) = _K  P(Next event | K) x P(K | Q)
[234] (Normal) 7  Structural Mapping Extension
[235] (Normal) 7.1 Motivation
[236] (Normal) Our approach utilizing embeddings only, might only match on surface words. Two incidents with “engine”, “oil”, or “failure” score a high similarity score, but their actual failure mechanism could be completely different. Furthermore, a maintenance error causing oil starvation and a fatigue crack causing an engine fire can look nearly identical to embeddings but are structurally very different incidents. To validate that our methodology doesn’t just match surface words, but actually are matching by the causes, we create a structural mapping approach that looks at two incidents and examines each part of these two incidents.
[237] (Normal) Embeddings similarity matches how similar texts are on the surface level. Two incidents can use similar verbiages of text but have completely different meanings. We want incidents whose causal structure matches the query, not just incidents that share similar texts. Structural mapping is a reranking of these embeddings to check the causal structure of each incident.
[238] (Normal) 7.2 Causal chain schema
[239] (Normal) An incident is represented as a causal chain. Each step has three fields: role, system, and mechanism. Role is where the step sits in the failure progression. System is looking at what exact part or system of the aircraft is this particular incident talking about. Mechanism is what happens as a result of the cause.
[240] (Normal) 7.3 LLM extraction
[241] (Normal) The causal chains are extracted by an LLM (gpt-4o-mini) from the incidents. Every step should have the same three fields (role, system, and mechanism). The query causal cause is also extracted the same way so they can compare between the incidents and the query. This process is cached, so the same chain is reused on later runs. We cache to keep the results reproducible, and we prevent them from increasing the LLM cost on every single run.
[245] (Normal) 7.4 Step Similarity
[246] (Normal) This step compares the query to the same step from the incident chain. We get a score between 0 and 1 for how similar these two steps are to one another. The score is then a weighted sum of the three fields.
[247] (Normal) Formula: sim (a, b) = 0.50 x role (a, b) + 0.30 x mechanism (a, b) + 0.20 x system (a, b)
[248] (Normal) The role step is weighted highest because this is where the step is in the failure progression matters the most. The mechanism step is the next highest weight because we find how the failure occurred. System is the last step that shows what failed. Each of the fields produces their own score between 0 to 1. An exact match would equal 1.0, related is partial match, and unrelated is not a match.
[249] (Normal) 7.5 Chain Alignment
[250] (Normal) The lengths of these causal chains have different lengths and are ordered differently. We need to figure out which step in the query chain lines up with the right step in the incident chain. So, we align the first two chains first and then score how well the alignment is. We use Needleman-Wunsch, a sequence alignment algorithm to find the best way to line up the two chains end to end. This algorithm also allows for gaps that the counterpart doesn’t have. Each gap is a penalty of subtracting 0.05. Lining up two steps is the step similarity score from section 7.4. The algorithm fills a grid: matching the two steps (add their step similarity) or take a gap in either chain (subtract 0.05).
[251] (Normal) The recurrence Formula 2:
[252] (Normal) D[i] [j] = max {
[253] (Normal) D[i-1] [j-1] + sim (a_i, b_j)		(match step i with step j)
[254] (Normal) D[i-1] [j] + gap penalty		(skip step i in chain A)
[255] (Normal) D[i] [j-1] + gap penalty		(skip step j in chain B)
[256] (Normal) }
[257] (Normal) *Sim (a_i, b_j) is the step similarity from section 7.4 and the gap penalty is subtraction 0.05
[258] (Normal) Formula 3 is scoring the alignment. Once aligned, we sort the matched parts by quality. Strong would mean that the chains are aligned with step similarity greater than or equal to 0.70. Partial means that the chains are aligned with the similarity greater than or equal to 0.35 and less than 0.70. Unmapped means both chains left with gaps. The total is the combined length of both chains.
[259] (Normal) Formula 3: Chain score = (Strong + 0.5 x Partial) / total – 0.10 x (unmapped / total)
[260] (Normal) *The results can be from 0-1.
[261] (Normal) * This structural similarity is between the query and that one incident. It’s what feeds into the fusion formula in section 7.6.
[262] (Normal) 7.6 Score Fusion
[263] (Normal) Now we have two similarity signals one from the embedding cosine score from section 6.3 explaining the surface/wording similarity and the structure similarity score from section 7.5 explaining the causal structure similarity. This section combines them into a new ranked score.
[264] (Normal) The Fusion Formula (Formula 4): new score = max (0, cosine) x exp(  x structure similarity), with  = 2.0
[265] (Normal) We take the cosine score and multiply by the exp(  x structure similarity). The structural similarity is between 0 and 1. The  equals 2.0 is controlling how strongly structural mapping matters. When structural similarity is equal to 0, exponent (0) equals 1, so the new score equals cosine A2(structural mapping) reduces exactly to A0 (the embedding baseline). Structural mapping can only boost an incident; it can’t put below its raw cosine score. Incidents whose causal structures match the query rise in the rerank. Next, we compute the new score for all top 50 retrieved incidents and rerank them by the new score. The reranked list feeds to the same clustering plus law of total probability step from section 6.

[333] (Normal) 9. Aggregate Validation
[334] (Normal) The examples worked in section 8 only cover two incidents. This section tests the pipeline on all 77 test incidents. We run both the embedding baseline (A0) and the structural mapping embedding (A2) on every test incident, comparing the top predicted cause against the NTSB probable causes. Then we report where the predictions land and how A2 compares to A0.
[335] (Normal) 9.1 Setup
[336] (Normal) We utilized a training and testing split of the NTSB data. From the subset of the incidents, we have both a complete narrative and probable cause findings of 254 incidents. We use 177 to train and build the retrieval index and 77 are held out for testing. For each of the test incidents, we use the narratives as the query and run the whole pipeline on both A0 (embedding only) and A2 (structural mapping), so the two pipelines are compared on the same incidents.
[337] (Normal) 9.2 Metric definition
[338] (Normal) The ground truth for an incident is the NTSB cause. For each prediction we take the top predicted cause, embed it, and measure cosine similarity to the NTSB probable-cause text; match percentage is that similarity times 100. In section 9.3 we report on our metrics.
[339] (List Bullet) Top-1 accuracy - the top prediction clears the match threshold (match percentage >= 75, i.e. cosine >= 0.75).
[340] (List Bullet) Recall@5 - a matching cause appears anywhere in the top 5 predictions.
[341] (List Bullet) Avg match% - mean similarity of the top prediction across all cases (a threshold-free view).

[357] (Normal) 9.5 Limitations of the Metric
[358] (Normal) The validation has several limitations. First, the hit threshold (cosine 0.75) is a modeling choice rather than a standard, so we report recall@5 and mean match percentage to keep the result from hinging on a single cutoff. Next, the same embedding model is used to retrieve incidents and for scoring predictions against the NTSB text. Furthermore, the test set is small (77 incidents). Having more test cases can help validate this process. Finally, this validation covers diagnosis only, prognosis is not validated because there are too few retrieved incidents that carry a recorded next event to support our metric, so prognosis is demonstrated qualitatively in the worked examples instead.
[360] (Normal) 10. Discussion
[361] (Normal) Below are a few observations from the tests. The first observation is that the cluster is at the right level to report a probability. The P(C | Q) probabilities come out small because the taxonomy is large. The probability is spread thin. The cluster probabilities are useful to see what area the issue has occurred in and help with decision making. The second observation is that the query length affects retrieval. Shorter failure focused queries retrieve better causes. The third observation is that the structural rerank is a small consistent positive. Looking at the data tested, the two pipelines showed similar results and agreed on 76 cases and disagreed on one, so we conclude that the structural mapping does not hurt the overall while keeping the causal chain alignment intact.
